The Art of Cementine: Restoring Original Sicilian Hydraulic Floor Tiles

By Arch. Fabio Costanzo, Studio 4e · Updated: March 2026

Urgent question? Call: +39 329 973 6697

Cementine siciliane — the hand-pressed hydraulic cement tiles with geometric Art Nouveau and Liberty patterns — are the most visually distinctive floor material in Sicilian historic buildings. Made in Palermo, Catania and Caltagirone workshops from the 1880s through the 1950s, they are the detail that distinguishes an authentic Sicilian interior from a reproduction. Restoring them correctly adds real value; replacing them with modern reproductions loses it.

What cementine actually are: materials and manufacturing

Hydraulic cement tiles (mattonelle idrauliche, cementine) are not fired — they are pressed. The process: a metal mould is filled with a thin layer of decorative pigmented cement paste (the face layer, 3–4mm), then backed with a layer of sand-cement mortar, then dry-pressed at high pressure (100–150 bar). The resulting tile — typically 20×20cm, 15–18mm thick — gains its colour from mineral oxides (iron oxide for reds and yellows, chromium oxide for greens, manganese for browns) bound in the cement face layer.

The tile's surface is the face layer itself, not a glaze — which means the colour goes a few millimetres deep and can be lightly ground back to fresh colour if surface staining is too deep to remove by cleaning. This is also why cementine are easy to crack: the face layer has no tensile strength and the tile fails when the substrate flexes.

Original Sicilian cementine from the 1880s–1940s typically measure 20×20cm with a 15–17mm thickness. A common pattern vocabulary: geometric star-and-cross patterns (stelle e croci), wave patterns (onde), floral rosettes (rosette), and border tiles (bordi) that combine with field tiles to create room-scale compositions. The best examples are in Palermo's Liberty-era apartment buildings (Viale della Libertà, Via Ruggiero Settimo) and in the masserie and case padronali of the Ragusa and Agrigento hinterlands.

Assessing the condition of existing cementine before starting

Before deciding on restoration vs replacement, assess:

  • Structural integrity: tap each tile with a wooden stick. A hollow sound (suono vuoto) indicates debonding from the substrate — the tile is at risk of cracking under foot traffic. Estimate the percentage of hollow tiles (over 30% hollow suggests substrate issues; under 10% can be reglued individually).
  • Surface condition: cementine become stained over decades from foot traffic, oil, and wax accumulation. Surface grime is almost always removable. Deep staining that has penetrated into the cement face (usually from prolonged water ingress with iron staining) is harder to address. Check for areas where the face layer has worn through to the backing mortar — this requires tile replacement rather than restoration.
  • Pattern completeness: assess how many tiles are broken, missing, or replaced with non-matching types. A floor with 5% broken tiles is a restoration candidate; a floor with 30% replaced or missing tiles may not be worth restoring if matching replacements are unavailable.
  • Substrate condition: probe the edges of loose tiles to assess the mortar bed beneath. If the mortar bed is loose, friable or contaminated with moisture, relaying the floor requires full removal and a new mortar bed.

The restoration process: cleaning, relaying and protecting

A professional cementine restoration on a 60 sqm floor (typical Sicilian apartment salon) involves:

  1. Deep cleaning (decapage): apply an alkaline degreaser followed by a diluted acid cleaner (3% hydrochloric or phosphoric acid) to remove wax build-up and surface grime. Rinse thoroughly. Cost: €8–15/sqm in professional restoration labour.
  2. Hollow tile stabilisation: inject specialist low-viscosity epoxy resin (resina epossidica bicomponente a bassa viscosità) under hollow tiles via drilled holes in the grout joints, then press down and allow to cure. This technique — called "consolidamento per iniezione" — reattaches up to 80% of hollow tiles without removal. Cost: €15–25 per tile stabilised.
  3. Tile replacement: broken, missing or irrecoverable tiles require replacement. Source matching tiles (see below) and lay in identical pattern orientation to the original. Use white cement-based adhesive (not grey — grey bleeds through the thin face layer) with a notched trowel at 3–4mm bed depth.
  4. Grout repair: regrout damaged joints with white hydraulic lime grout (not Portland cement, which is too hard and cracks around tiles with the building's normal thermal movement).
  5. Sealing: apply a penetrating silicone-based impregnator (not a surface coating) after cleaning. The sealer prevents future oil and water staining while allowing the tile to breathe. Reapply every 3–5 years in kitchen and bathroom contexts.

Sourcing replacement tiles: where to find matching cementine

Finding matching original cementine is the hardest part of any restoration. Three sources:

  • Antique dealers and salvage yards: Palermo's Mercato delle Pulci at Piazza Peranni and the salvage dealers on Via Buonriposo occasionally have batches of original cementine salvaged from demolitions. Prices: €2–8 per tile for common patterns, €10–25 for rare patterns. The challenge: finding enough tiles of exactly the right pattern and colour to complete a specific room can take months of searching.
  • Contemporary artisan manufacturers: several Sicilian workshops produce faithful reproductions using original hydraulic press techniques. The best-known: Cementine di Sicilia (Palermo), Ceramiche Faenza di Caltagirone (which produces cementine alongside its ceramic production), and smaller ateliers in the Palermo and Catania hinterlands. Price: €18–35/sqm for standard patterns, €45–80/sqm for custom pattern matches. Lead time: 8–16 weeks for custom orders.
  • International suppliers: companies like Marrakech Design (Sweden), Ege Seramik (Turkey) and Nanda Tiles (India) produce hydraulic cement tiles in Sicilian-inspired patterns for the export market. Tile dimensions may differ slightly from original Sicilian sizes. Best for uniform field areas where exact pattern match is not critical.

For a restoration where exact pattern matching matters (room borders, named-pattern compositions), work with a Palermo-based artigiano manufacturer who can analyse an existing tile sample and produce a batch-matched reproduction. Commission a test batch of 20 tiles before ordering the full quantity.

Cementine over underfloor heating: the compatibility question

Hydraulic cement tiles have a thermal expansion coefficient similar to the mortar bed (≈ 10 × 10⁻⁶ per °C). They can be laid over low-temperature underfloor heating (max 28°C floor surface) if the following conditions are met: flexible adhesive (≥ S1 class per EN 12002), expansion joints at perimeter and at maximum 5m intervals in each direction, and maximum flow temperature of 40°C in the UFH circuit. Higher temperatures cause differential expansion that cracks the tile face layer at the grout joints.

For an existing floor with original cementine: installing UFH beneath requires lifting and relaying the floor — a significant operation that risks damaging tiles during removal. Assess whether the aesthetic benefit of UFH justifies the risk and cost (typically €60–90/sqm for lift, substrate preparation, UFH installation, and refix). For floors with irreplaceable 19th-century tiles, the risk is usually not worth taking.

Adding value vs reducing it: the interior designer's perspective

In the current Sicilian luxury property market (2024–2026), original cementine in excellent restored condition add 8–15% to the assessed value of an historic apartment relative to an identical apartment with modern tile floors. Buyers from northern Europe and North America specifically request properties with original historic floor materials — they are a primary selling point, not an obstacle to modernisation.

The most value-destroying decision: replacing original cementine with large-format porcelain tiles (60×60 or 60×120) in an attempt to "modernise" the property. This decision is essentially irreversible (the cementine are destroyed) and removes the distinctive quality that drives premium valuations. Studio 4e's standard advice: restore original cementine wherever feasible, even at higher short-term cost, because the long-term value preservation is clear.
