Smart Home Technology in 18th-Century Palazzos: A Hidden Integration Guide

By Arch. Fabio Costanzo, Studio 4e · Updated: March 2026

Urgent question? Call: +39 329 973 6697

Integrating smart home automation into an 18th-century Sicilian palazzo is primarily an infrastructure concealment problem. KNX bus cables, AV distribution runs, motor-driven shutter wiring, and HVAC control cabling must be routed through buildings where the walls are 70cm of solid stone, the ceilings are frescoed vaults, and the Soprintendenza requires that no visible surface-mounted cabling be accepted. The solution is to plan the infrastructure before the plaster is applied — which means deciding on automation before the renovation specification is finalised.

The golden rule: automation infrastructure is a rough-works decision

The single most important rule for smart home integration in a historic building: every cable route, every conduit, every in-wall backbox must be planned and installed during the rustico phase (structural and rough works), before internal plasters are applied. Installing automation retrospectively in a finished historic palazzo means either cutting channels in protected plaster (which the Soprintendenza may prohibit) or running surface-mounted conduit (which destroys the aesthetic).

This has a practical project management implication: the automation consultant must be engaged before the renovation specification is finalised — not after the plasterer has finished and the client has decided they want motorised shutters. Changing the cable routing after plastering on a protected building costs 3–5× more than doing it correctly in the rough-works phase, and is sometimes simply not possible.

KNX vs proprietary systems: which works best in stone buildings

The two dominant control protocols for high-end residential smart home in Europe are KNX (the open European standard, ISO 22510) and proprietary systems (Crestron, Control4, Savant, Lutron). In a Sicilian historic palazzo, KNX has specific advantages:

  • Twisted-pair cable infrastructure: KNX devices communicate over a simple 2-wire bus cable (YCYM 2×2×0.8mm). One cable loop can connect up to 64 devices on a segment — which means a single cable route from the plant room to each floor can serve all the automation needs on that floor. In a stone building with limited wall cavity space, fewer cable runs = more achievable infrastructure.
  • Distributed intelligence: KNX devices contain their own intelligence — a KNX switch knows to send a telegram when pressed, regardless of the central controller's status. If the central server fails, local control still works. This is important in Sicilian properties that are vacant for periods — you don't want the shutters stuck because the server crashed during a storm.
  • Local Italian installer base: KNX certification training is widespread in Italy. Finding a Sicilian KNX integrator is substantially easier than finding a Crestron or Control4 programmer in Sicily — important for ongoing maintenance when you are abroad.

Proprietary systems (Crestron, Control4) offer better GUI quality and more sophisticated AV control but require factory-certified programmers who are rare in Sicily. For a palazzo primarily used for audio-visual entertainment with complex multi-room AV, a proprietary system is justifiable. For a residence primarily requiring lighting, HVAC, and security integration, KNX with an IP-based touch panel interface is typically sufficient and more maintainable.

Cable concealment techniques in stone walls

The practical cable routing options in a stone palazzo:

  • Horizontal chases in the plaster (tracce nell'intonaco): shallow channels cut into the external plaster layer (10–20mm deep) to conceal cables before replastering. Acceptable for thin KNX bus cables or single circuits. Not acceptable for bundles of larger cables. Requires Soprintendenza consultation if the plaster being cut is original.
  • Vertical shafts via brickwork joints: where walls have mortar joints (as opposed to pure stone), cables can be passed vertically through drilled mortar joints with minimal disruption to masonry. Limited to small-diameter cables (up to 10mm).
  • False floor / raised access floor: installing a 50–80mm raised floor over the original floor surface (protected with a temporary covering) provides a horizontal cable distribution zone. Vertical risers from this zone can be concealed in service shafts or within new internal wall partitions. This is the most flexible cable routing solution and is common in high-specification renovation projects where budget allows.
  • New internal partition walls: introducing new stud partition walls (pareti in cartongesso or light masonry) adjacent to the historic stone walls creates a cavity for cable routing. The partition must be clearly identifiable as new construction (different material from the original) which is architecturally honest and usually acceptable to the Soprintendenza.
  • Ceiling cornice channels: in rooms with elaborate stucco cornices, a plasterboard-enclosed cable channel can be integrated behind the cornice without damage to the original plasterwork. The new plasterboard element is designed to match the cornice profile from the room view but conceals cabling in the zone between the new element and the original wall.

Motorised shutters in protected windows: the key challenge

Motorised shutters (tapparelle elettriche or persiane motorizzate) are one of the most requested automation features in Sicilian palazzi. The challenge: the Soprintendenza typically requires that windows in classified buildings maintain their original profile and operation — meaning the traditional wooden persiane shutters on hinges, not the roller shutters common in modern Italian construction.

The solution that works: motorised hinged persiana hardware (motorini elettrici per persiane a battente) drives the original wooden shutter leaf via a concealed motor installed in the window reveal. Motor dimensions are small enough (80×30mm typical) to fit in the existing window reveal without modification visible from the street. The motor connects to the KNX bus via a 2-wire cable concealed in the wall chase.

Motor brands suitable for historic window applications: Gaposa (Italian), Selve (German), Somfy (French). Cost: €250–400 per window including motor, controller, and KNX interface. For a palazzo with 20 shuttered windows, the total shutter motorisation cost (motors + cabling + programming): €8,000–14,000.

AV distribution in a multi-room palazzo

Multi-room audio and video distribution in a stone building requires dedicated cabling from a central AV distribution point to each room. Key infrastructure decisions:

  • HDMI over Cat6 extenders vs distributed video matrix: HDMI runs over standard Cat6 twisted pair (up to 70–100m per run) using HDBaseT extenders. This means Cat6 cable — which is easily routed through wall chases — serves as the video distribution backbone. A matrix switcher at the plant room allows any source to be sent to any display. This approach is significantly easier to install in an existing building than home-run HDMI cables (which are rigid and hard to pull through bends).
  • Structured speaker cabling: in-ceiling speakers require 2-core speaker cable from each speaker position back to the amplifier. In a vaulted space without false ceilings, surface-mounted ceiling speakers are usually impossible without damaging the vault. Options: flush-mounted speakers in a new plasterboard suspended ceiling element (designed as part of the interior design), or a high-quality freestanding speaker system that requires no in-wall cabling.
  • Ethernet and WiFi infrastructure: CAT6A or CAT7 ethernet drops in each room, connected to a WiFi access point behind a discrete wall panel, are the most reliable wireless networking solution. Mesh WiFi systems that rely on wireless backhaul have poor performance through 70cm stone walls — wired ethernet backhaul to each AP is the correct specification.

Access control and security in a historic palazzo

Modern access control (video doorbell, electronic entry lock, camera system) must be integrated without visually compromising the historic facade. Approaches that work:

  • Video doorbell: a flush-mounted audio-video citofono in a recess within the existing wall thickness at the main entrance. Preferred brand: BTicino or Urmet (Italian market leaders with products sized for traditional door surrounds)
  • Door lock: an electronic lock mechanism (Iseo, Mul-T-Lock, or Abloy) installed within the existing door hardware profile — invisible externally
  • Cameras: discreet PTZ cameras in building fabric corners, facing outward. For classified buildings, any external camera installation requires a parere from the Soprintendenza — document the position and specification in the permit application

Complete smart home specification for a 400 sqm palazzo (4 floors, 8 rooms, full KNX integration, motorised shutters, multi-room AV, access control, HVAC integration): €45,000–90,000 in equipment and programming, plus infrastructure installation costs within the main renovation contract (cabling, conduit, plant room: €12,000–22,000). Total automation investment: €60,000–110,000 for a thorough specification.
