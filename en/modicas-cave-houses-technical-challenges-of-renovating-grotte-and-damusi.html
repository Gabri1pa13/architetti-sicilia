Modica's Cave Houses: Technical Challenges of Renovating Grotte and Damusi

By Arch. Fabio Costanzo, Studio 4e · Updated: March 2026

Modica's Ibla district contains some of the most unusual residential properties in Sicily — case grotta (cave dwellings) excavated directly into the limestone rock face, with rooms carved from the living stone rather than built in masonry. These properties offer extreme thermal stability, authentic Ibleo character, and renovation challenges that no standard building survey or architect's experience prepares you for.

What cave dwellings actually are in Modica's context

The grotte of Modica (and Ispica, Scicli, and the Iblean cave district generally) are natural or semi-natural cavities in the Iblean limestone that were used for habitation from prehistoric times through to the early 20th century. Three categories exist:

  • Pure grotte (cave rooms): rooms entirely carved from limestone with no added masonry construction. Wall, floor and ceiling are all rock. Very few survive in a habitable state — most were abandoned when post-1693 baroque construction made above-ground housing the norm.
  • Case grotta miste (mixed cave-masonry houses): the most common type — a limestone rock face provides the rear wall and sometimes the ceiling of one or more rooms, with masonry walls on the exposed faces. The cave section provides a semi-basement or ground floor, with a masonry extension above or in front. This is the standard "cave house" of Modica's market.
  • Damusi ipogei (underground stores converted to habitation): former agricultural stores (olive oil presses, wine cellars, animal housing) in the basement level of above-ground buildings. These have masonry vaulted ceilings but stone-carved floor and lower walls.

For planning and Soprintendenza purposes, all three categories fall within the Comune di Modica's centro storico PRG Zone A and are subject to the Soprintendenza di Ragusa's oversight. Many have archaeological significance (Neolithic, Bronze Age, or Siculo-Roman occupation evidence) which triggers a procedura di tutela archaeologica — any excavation within or adjacent to the cave requires an archaeologist's presence.

The humidity problem: geology and hydrology of the Iblean limestone

Iblean limestone (calcarenite iblea) is a porous bioclastic sedimentary rock with high water absorption. Rainwater percolates through the overlying limestone hillside and seeps through cracks and pores to emerge in cave walls, floors and ceilings. The hydraulic gradient depends on the hillside above, the seasonal rainfall, and whether any drainage works have been carried out at higher levels.

In many Modica grotte, seepage from the hillside means that the cave walls and floor are permanently damp — not from flooding, but from slow continuous seepage. Humidity levels of 80–95% are typical in unheated cave rooms in autumn-winter. At these humidity levels, organic materials deteriorate rapidly, electrical equipment fails prematurely, and respiratory health risks are significant for permanent occupants.

The remediation approach: a combination of external drainage (intercept rainwater at the hillside above the cave), internal drainage channels cut around the perimeter of the cave floor, waterproof render on the lower cave walls (cocciopesto or modern hydraulic lime), and mechanical ventilation. This works well when designed correctly but requires a hydrological analysis of the hillside above to identify the water sources. Studio 4e's protocol: commission a hydrogeological survey before designing the drainage system, not after.

Ventilation: carbon dioxide and radon in cave spaces

Cave rooms without mechanical ventilation accumulate CO₂ from normal occupant respiration faster than equivalent above-ground rooms — because the rock walls do not allow air exchange, and the limited window openings restrict natural convection. In a 20 sqm cave room with two occupants and no mechanical ventilation, CO₂ can exceed 1,500 ppm within 2 hours — above the 1,000 ppm level recommended for cognitive performance.

Radon — a naturally occurring radioactive gas that seeps from limestone and granite bedrock — is a specific concern in Iblean cave properties. The Regione Siciliana's radon map identifies the Ibleo limestone zone as moderate-to-high radon potential. Italian law (D.Lgs 101/2020) requires radon measurement in residential spaces when the property owner has reason to suspect elevated levels, and requires remediation if measured annual average radon concentration exceeds 200 Bq/m³ (new buildings) or 300 Bq/m³ (existing buildings).

Radon measurement costs: €200–400 for a 90-day measurement using passive radon detectors. If radon exceeds limits, the remediation approach (sub-slab depressurization, mechanical ventilation) costs €3,000–8,000 for a cave dwelling. This should be commissioned before finalising the renovation brief, not after — it affects the entire ventilation system design.

Structural assessment specific to cave dwellings

The structural system of a case grotta miste is unlike any conventional building assessment scenario:

  • Rock face integrity: the limestone rock that forms the cave walls and ceiling must be assessed for stability. Loose blocks (massi pericolanti), solution cavities behind the visible surface, and joint-controlled block failure are the three main structural risks. An engineering geologist (ingegnere geologo) rather than a standard structural engineer should lead this assessment.
  • Masonry-rock interface: where a masonry wall meets the limestone rock face, differential movement between the two materials creates recurring cracking. The joint must be designed with a flexible filler (polyurethane sealant on a backer rod) rather than a rigid mortar joint.
  • Overburden loading: the weight of the hill above the cave ceiling creates a significant surcharge. If the hillside above is being built on, or if existing buildings above are being extended, the additional load may exceed the rock ceiling's capacity. Always commission a rock mechanics assessment if any loading changes are proposed above the cave.
  • Water penetration and dissolution: rainwater percolating through limestone slowly dissolves the rock. Over 100+ years of a seeping grotta, significant dissolution can widen joints and thin ceiling elements. Core sampling of the ceiling at 3–4 points determines whether the ceiling thickness is adequate.

The thermal advantage: why cave houses are genuinely excellent in Sicilian summers

Despite their humidity and ventilation challenges, cave rooms offer genuine thermal advantages that justify their renovation complexity:

  • Mean annual cave temperature in Modica's limestone: approximately 16–17°C. This is the stable "deep earth" temperature at 5–10m below grade.
  • Summer peak temperature in a well-dried and ventilated cave room: 18–22°C — requiring no air conditioning even during 38°C summer days above ground.
  • Winter minimum in a cave room: 14–16°C — requiring relatively little heating to reach comfort temperature (18°C) compared to an equivalent above-ground room with thin walls.

These thermal characteristics explain the historical use of cave rooms as summer refugia (the noble families of the Ibleo plateau retreated to their cave-level reception rooms during July and August), and make cave rooms highly appealing to international holiday renters seeking authentic coolness without air conditioning.

Budget expectations for a cave house renovation

All-in costs for a 60 sqm case grotta miste renovation in Modica:

  • Hydrogeological survey + radon measurement + engineering geology assessment: €3,500–6,000
  • Rock face stabilisation (consolidamento della parete): €8,000–18,000 depending on condition
  • Drainage and waterproofing system: €6,000–14,000
  • Mechanical ventilation (MVHR) with radon extraction: €5,000–9,000
  • Standard renovation (impianti, finishes, kitchen, bathroom): €45,000–70,000
  • Professional fees + permits: €6,000–12,000
  • Total: €75,000–130,000 all-in for a habitable, legally registered cave dwelling
