Reinforcing Foundations: Underpinning Historic Buildings on Clay Soil in Sicily

By Arch. Fabio Costanzo, Studio 4e · Updated: March 2026

Urgent question? Call: +39 329 973 6697

The plastic clays (argille azzurre) that underlie large parts of Sicily's interior — particularly in Agrigento, Caltanissetta, Enna, and inland Ragusa provinces — are among the most problematic foundation soils in Europe. They shrink in dry seasons and swell when wet, causing differential settlement that cracks historic masonry, rotates walls, and in severe cases threatens structural collapse. Underpinning is the solution — but it is complex, expensive, and requires a structural engineer with specific soil mechanics expertise.

Why Sicilian clay soils cause so much foundation movement

The argille azzurre (literally "blue clays") of Sicily's interior are Plio-Pleistocene marine clays with high plasticity index (IP 30–60%) and significant shrink-swell potential. During Sicily's hot dry summers (June–September), surface soil moisture evaporates deeply — sometimes to 2–3 metres depth — and the clay consolidates, causing downward settlement. During autumn-winter rains, the clay absorbs water, swells, and heaves upward. This annual cycle of consolidation and heave creates differential movement that historic masonry — which has little tensile strength — cannot absorb without cracking.

The problem is exacerbated by two factors common to rural Sicilian properties: shallow original foundations (many 19th-century rural buildings have foundations of only 400–600mm below grade, which is entirely within the active shrink-swell zone), and the presence of trees and vegetation near the building (tree roots extract moisture, creating localised dry zones beneath the foundation that worsen differential settlement).

Identifying foundation movement vs other structural problems

Not all cracks in Sicilian buildings indicate foundation movement. The structural engineer's first task is to distinguish foundation movement (settlement or heave) from seismic damage, thermal movement, or past construction defects. The diagnostic indicators of foundation movement:

  • Diagonal cracks from corners of openings: the classic "staircase crack" following mortar joints diagonally, typically running from the corner of a door or window downward toward a foundation corner. Indicates differential settlement with the wall rotating about a point near the settled foundation.
  • Floor level monitoring: a precise spirit level survey across the floors reveals which areas of the building have settled or heaved. Clay-related movement typically produces a gradual bowl or tilt pattern across the floor rather than the localised sudden drops of foundation failure.
  • Crack pattern symmetry: clay movement cracks often have seasonal behaviour — cracks that open in summer and close in winter are a strong indicator of shrink-swell activity. Ask the seller or tenant whether cracks "move with the seasons."
  • Soil investigation: a geotechnical investigation (3–4 boreholes or dynamic probes to 5–8m depth) confirms soil type and identifies the depth of the shrink-swell zone. This is mandatory before any underpinning design and costs €3,000–6,000 for a standard rural property investigation.

The three main underpinning techniques used in Sicily

1. Traditional pit underpinning (sottofondazione tradizionale)

Excavation of sequential bays beneath the existing foundation, each bay then filled with reinforced concrete before the next is opened. The process transfers load progressively to deeper, more stable soil strata. Suitable for buildings with modest loads (rural farmhouses, 2-storey masonry) on clay soils where competent strata are within 2–3m of the original foundation. Cost: €600–1,000 per linear metre of wall underpinned. For a 4-sided building requiring underpinning on two walls (30m): €18,000–30,000.

2. Mini-pile underpinning (micropali)

Drilling slender reinforced concrete piles (100–250mm diameter) through the existing foundation and clay layer to reach competent rock or dense gravel/sand below. The piles are capped with a reinforced concrete needle beam that transfers the foundation load. Suitable for: buildings on deep clay (competent material below 3m), buildings requiring minimal disturbance during works, buildings where access for traditional excavation equipment is impossible (dense urban contexts, narrow streets). Cost: €2,500–4,500 per pile; a typical underpinning scheme may require 15–30 piles. Total: €40,000–120,000 depending on scope.

3. Soil improvement (consolidamento del suolo)

Injection of expansive cement-based grouts (jet grouting or compaction grouting) into the clay layer to increase its stiffness and reduce future movement potential. Less invasive than traditional underpinning. Suitable for: buildings with ongoing active movement where the mechanism is primarily clay swelling rather than deep-seated settlement. Not suitable for: cases where the competent layer is more than 4m below the foundation level. Cost: €8,000–20,000 for a standard rural property treatment. Requires specialist contractor with grouting equipment — not all Sicilian structural firms have this capability.

The anti-seismic upgrade interaction

Any underpinning or foundation reinforcement project in Sicily triggers the requirement to assess the building's compliance with NTC 2018 seismic norms. The Genio Civile deposit of the structural project must include a seismic vulnerability assessment (valutazione della vulnerabilità sismica). If the assessment identifies the building as seismically inadequate (Class D or E vulnerability), the permit may require seismic upgrading as a condition of approval for the underpinning works.

This creates a scope expansion risk: a project that starts as foundation stabilisation can be required to incorporate seismic strengthening (for example, tie rods connecting opposite walls, RC collar beams at floor and roof level, or shotcrete wall reinforcement). For a 150 sqm rural farmhouse, seismic upgrading adds €30,000–60,000 to the foundation stabilisation cost. Budget conservatively when any foundation works are planned in clay soil areas of Sicily.

Finding a structural engineer with specific soil mechanics expertise

Foundation problems in expansive clay are a specialist field within structural engineering. Not every ingegnere civile has the soil mechanics background to correctly diagnose clay movement and specify the appropriate intervention. Look for:

  • An engineer with documented experience in Sicily's argille azzurre zone (ask for reference projects in Agrigento, Caltanissetta, or Enna provinces)
  • Membership of the Ordine degli Ingegneri with specific geotechnical training (certificazione UNI EN ISO 22476 or equivalent)
  • Willingness to commission a formal geotechnical investigation (soil borings) rather than relying on visual inspection alone

Red flag: a contractor who offers to "inject some resin under the foundations" without a geotechnical investigation and engineering report. Chemical injection can stabilise foundations under some conditions, but applied without diagnosis it may be ineffective, or worse, may increase movement by changing the moisture balance beneath the building.

Indicative total project cost for a clay-soil foundation problem

  • Geotechnical investigation: €3,000–6,000
  • Structural engineering design and Genio Civile submission: €5,000–12,000
  • Underpinning works (depending on technique): €20,000–80,000
  • Associated crack repair and structural plaster restoration: €8,000–20,000
  • Seismic upgrading (if required by assessment): €30,000–60,000
  • Total range: €40,000–160,000 depending on building size and severity of movement

This is one of the few renovation types where there is no realistic "budget" option — inadequate foundation stabilisation will fail, often quickly and expensively. The cost of getting it wrong (renewed cracking, continued movement, potential structural collapse requiring full demolition and rebuild) far exceeds the cost of the correct engineering approach from the outset.
