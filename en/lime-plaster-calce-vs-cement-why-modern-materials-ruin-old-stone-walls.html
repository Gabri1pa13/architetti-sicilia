Lime Plaster vs Cement: Why Modern Materials Ruin Old Stone Walls

By Arch. Fabio Costanzo, Studio 4e · Updated: March 2026

Urgent question? Call: +39 329 973 6697

The single most common and most damaging material error in Sicilian historic building renovation is applying modern Portland cement render to old stone masonry walls. Within 5–15 years, this causes visible damage: spalling, efflorescence, trapped moisture, structural crack propagation. It is expensive and painful to correct, and the damage to historic fabric is sometimes irreversible. Understanding why — and how to specify the correct lime-based alternative — is fundamental knowledge for any renovation in a Sicilian stone building.

The physics: why cement fails on old masonry

Portland cement render has two characteristics that make it incompatible with historic stone masonry:

1. High strength and stiffness: modern cement mortars have compressive strength of 25–40 MPa. Historic Sicilian limestone (calcarenite, tufa, lava stone) has compressive strength of 10–30 MPa — often weaker than the cement applied to it. When thermal movement or structural settlement causes cracking, the failure occurs in the weaker stone rather than the stronger cement layer. A flexible, weaker mortar (traditional lime) would crack at the mortar joint and be easily repaired; rigid cement transfers stress to the stone and damages it.

2. Low vapour permeability: cement render has a vapour diffusion resistance (µ value) of 30–60, compared to 5–10 for traditional lime render. This means that moisture — rising from the ground through the wall, or absorbed from driving rain — cannot evaporate through the cement layer and is trapped inside the wall. The moisture accumulates, cycles through freeze-thaw in winter (causing spalling), carries soluble salts to the surface (efflorescence), and raises the wall's thermal conductivity (making it feel damp and cold).

The combination of these two properties explains the characteristic failure pattern seen on cemented Sicilian walls: patches of cement detaching from the stone with a damp, powdery layer beneath; white crystalline salt deposits on the cement surface; and cracks following the original stone coursing beneath the cement.

Lime plaster: the traditional solution and its properties

Traditional Sicilian render is based on calce aerea (air lime, primarily calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂) or calce idraulica naturale (natural hydraulic lime, NHL) mixed with local aggregates (river sand, volcanic sand from Etna, or crushed limestone). These mortars have:

  • Low compressive strength: 1–8 MPa (NHL 2 to NHL 5 grades per EN 459), weaker than the stone they protect. Failure at mortar joints is easily repaired.
  • High vapour permeability: µ value of 5–10, allowing moisture to pass through and evaporate. Walls stay drier internally.
  • Flexibility (creep): lime mortars creep slightly over time, accommodating micro-movements without cracking.
  • Self-healing (autogenous repair): hairline cracks in lime mortar partially heal when calcium carbonate recarbonates in the presence of atmospheric CO₂ and moisture — a maintenance advantage not shared by cement.

The natural hydraulic lime grades (NHL 2, NHL 3.5, NHL 5) are calibrated for different applications. NHL 2 (low hydraulic activity) is used for interior plasters and sheltered exteriors. NHL 3.5 is the standard exterior render for Sicilian building exteriors. NHL 5 (higher hydraulic activity) is used in wet applications (below damp-proof course level, cisterns) where faster setting is needed.

Identifying the correct lime mortar specification

Not all lime products are equal. The Sicilian building merchant who sells "calce" may be selling:

  • Pure air lime (calce aerea CL 90, EN 459): very slow hardening, lowest strength, best vapour permeability. Suitable for interior plasters and backgrounds.
  • Natural hydraulic lime (NHL, EN 459): moderate strength, good vapour permeability, the standard for Sicilian exterior applications.
  • Formulated lime-cement blends (often marketed as "calce bastarda" or "calce per intonaci"): contain Portland cement additions (5–25%) for faster setting and higher strength. Partially defeats the purpose — use only where faster setting is operationally necessary (winter works).
  • Factory-produced lime-based restoration mortars (Malta per restauro): pre-blended mortars specifically formulated for historic building work. Products from Mapei (Malta di Calce), Kerakoll (BioCalce Intonaco), and Tassullo (Antique) are used in professional restoration contracts. More expensive (€15–25/25kg bag vs €5–8 for basic NHL) but consistently formulated and tested.

For a Sicilian exterior render, the minimum specification: NHL 3.5 lime (EN 459 certified, not calce bastarda) mixed with clean coarse aggregate at 1:3 (lime:sand by volume), applied in two layers (arriccio 15mm + finitura 8mm), cured slowly under damp conditions for minimum 28 days.

Removing existing cement render: when and how

If cement render is already on a stone wall, the decision to remove it depends on its condition and the building's planned future treatment:

  • Remove now: if the cement is cracked, detaching, or causing visible moisture damage (efflorescence, staining, spalling stone behind). Removal is urgent to prevent further deterioration.
  • Leave temporarily: if the cement is intact, well-adhered, and the wall surface is dry, the cement can remain for the duration of the existing building use and be removed at the next significant renovation. Forcing removal from a stable cement render damages more fabric than leaving it in place short-term.

Cement removal technique: light pneumatic chiselling (martello pneumatico leggero), water jet (where the substrate is robust limestone), or chemical poultice in areas near frescoes or delicate finishes. Never diamond cutting or angle grinding — the vibration damages the stone joints behind. Lime mortar joint pointing (stilatura) must be renewed after cement removal, using NHL 3.5 pointing mortar.

Cost of cement render removal and lime replastering: €35–65/sqm for external walls depending on accessibility and stone surface condition. A full exterior replastering of a 150 sqm farmhouse (300 sqm of external wall area): €10,500–19,500 — expensive but typically cheaper than the structural repairs caused by 10 more years of moisture entrapment.

The Soprintendenza requirement: lime is almost always mandatory for classified buildings

The Soprintendenza's technical specifications for classified buildings uniformly require lime-based mortars for all external renders and internal plasters. In practice, any contractor who proposes cement render for a Soprintendenza-approved restoration contract should be immediately questioned — it indicates either inexperience with conservation practice or a cost-cutting proposal that will fail Soprintendenza inspection.

Where specification disputes arise between owners, contractors, and Soprintendenza: the Soprintendenza's position is unambiguous and binding. The contractor who argues for cement render because it is "faster" or "stronger" is arguing a case they will lose in the permit process. Any additional cost of lime-based materials and slower application is a project cost, not an optional upgrade.

The practical training gap: finding lime plaster specialists in Sicily

Traditional lime plasterwork is a skilled trade that requires specific knowledge — mixing consistency, application technique, curing management — that differs substantially from cement render work. In Sicily, the generation of builders trained in lime plaster is ageing out; many younger builders have only worked with cement and gypsum. Finding a muratore esperto in calce tradizionale for a historic building project requires:

  • Asking the Soprintendenza's approved contractor register for plasterers with conservation credentials
  • Checking with lime product suppliers (Tassullo, Kerakoll, Mapei) for their certified installer networks in Sicily
  • Asking other architects who work in the conservation sector for recommendations

The specialist premium for a qualified lime plasterer over a standard builder is typically 30–50% per day. On a total render budget of €15,000, this premium is €4,500–7,500 — a significant but necessary cost for work that will be inspected by the Soprintendenza and that will either last 50 years or fail in 5.
